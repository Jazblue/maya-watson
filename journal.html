Journal

Short thoughts and observations

These are the kinds of things I might text a friend, mutter to myself while waiting for the kettle to boil, or actually write down if I'm feeling particularly reflective. Think of them as snapshots of my mind rather than polished essays.

Topics range from tennis frustrations to London discoveries, from things that make me laugh to the quiet moments that somehow stick with you.

October 30, 2026

The Rain That Finally Shut Me Up

Caught in that typical October drizzle this afternoon — the kind that starts as a mist and ends as a proper downpour without bothering to warn you. Matt insisted we take the long way home along the river, which I protested loudly until he pointed out I'd been complaining about the weather for three days straight. So we went, two sensible Londoners who've learned not to let a bit of water slow us down.

The Thames looked completely different under the grey sky — moody and dramatic, like it was auditioning for a noir film. I had my camera tucked under my coat, pulling it out every few minutes to catch the way the rain made the city's reflections shimmer. Matt kept pulling his hood up and muttering about my "fair-weather photography," but he didn't suggest we cut the walk short, which I took as a win.

By the time we got home, we were both damp through and laughing about how we'd end up with the most atmospheric photos we've taken all year. Sometimes the best shots come from the weather you spend all week complaining about. And sometimes the best walks are the ones you take because someone knows exactly how to make you stop grumbling and just enjoy being in the city you love.

— The rain's still falling. I'm glad we didn't let it win.

October 29, 2026

Sunday Morning, and the Way He Reads the Paper

Sunday mornings at our place have a particular rhythm — Matt claims the paper, I claim the camera, and we orbit each other like we've been doing it for eleven years, which, I suppose, we have. This morning the light was doing that soft October thing, filtering through the kitchen window and catching the steam from his tea in a way that made me lift the camera without thinking. He didn't even look up, just kept reading, occasionally muttering about the football results in that tone he uses when he's pretending to be grumpy but is actually just comfortably content.

I took a few shots — his profile against the window, the way his reading glasses sit slightly down his nose, the concentration line between his brows when he's working through a particularly tedious op-ed. He's not a patient man in most things, but with the Sunday paper, he's a monk. There's something deeply attractive about watching someone be completely, unselfconsciously absorbed in something they love.

Later, when he finally folded the paper and looked up, I was still clicking away. He raised an eyebrow and said, "Are you documenting my descent into middle-aged grumpiness?" I told him I was documenting the part where he still looks like the man I fell for, just with better hair and worse opinions about politics. He laughed, kissed my forehead, and asked if I wanted tea. I said yes, and he made it exactly how I like it — two sugars, splash of milk, no discussion.

— Some of my favourite photographs are the ones where he doesn't know I'm taking them.

October 28, 2026

The Day I Finally Admitted I'm a Tennis Loser

Played doubles this morning — the usual Thursday crew at the local club, all of us pretending we're still thirty-five and not, in my case, fifty-two with knees that make suspicious clicking noises when I bend down. I lost. Again. Not in a dramatic, match-point, heartbreak way — more in a slow, steady, "how did I end up here again?" kind of way. My partner, Sarah, spent most of the second set looking at me like I'd forgotten how to hold a racket, which, honestly, I had about six times.

Matt was waiting in the car when I finally trudged out, sweat dripping off my chin like I'd been swimming instead of failing at a sport I claim to love. He took one look at me, handed over a bottle of water without a word, and said, "So, how many times do I have to tell you — the secret to tennis is not trying too hard?" I told him the secret to tennis is actually playing it, and he just grinned like he knew exactly what I meant. He's been watching me lose at this particular hobby for three years now. I think he's developed a sort of affectionate pity for my competitive delusions.

Here's the thing about being 52 and still terrible at tennis: you know exactly what you're doing wrong and you still can't fix it. The backhand is a mystery. The serve goes into the net more often than it goes over. And yet, I'll be back next Thursday. Because losing badly is still better than not playing at all. And because somewhere between the bad shots and the sore muscles, I found something I'd forgotten — that joy isn't always about winning. Sometimes it's just about showing up, swinging, and laughing at yourself in the car on the way home.

— Still terrible. Still going back. Still loving it.

October 27, 2026

The Morning I Let the Leaf Win

I was out with my camera at dawn, determined to catch that precise moment when the autumn light hits the plane trees along our street. You know the shot — gold leaves against the grey London sky, that slightly theatrical glow that makes everything look like it's auditioning for a period drama. I'd been planning it for days.

Matt, bless him, watched me set up with the patience of a man who's seen this particular obsession play out at least fifty times. "You know," he said, handing me a coffee that was probably his third, "the tree's not going anywhere." I told him I wasn't chasing the tree, I was chasing the light. He raised an eyebrow and retreated to the kitchen, muttering something about photographers being the most impatient patient people he knows.

The light never quite materialised. The clouds shifted, the wind picked up, and after forty minutes of adjusting aperture and muttering about exposure, I packed the camera away. The leaf I'd been tracking — a stubborn red thing clinging to the highest branch — stayed exactly where it was, smugly unphotographed.

Matt appeared beside me on the bench, took one look at my expression, and said, "So, the leaf won, then?" I laughed despite myself. "The leaf was never the competition," I said. "It was me, trying to force autumn to pose."

Sometimes the best thing you can do with a camera is put it down. And sometimes the best thing you can do with a Tuesday morning is just sit on a bench with someone who knows exactly why you're frustrated and doesn't mind waiting for you to get over it.

— The leaf's still up there. I think it's staying.

The Day I Realised I'd Become a London Landmark

Took Matt for a walk along the South Bank this afternoon — the kind of stroll where you don't need to go anywhere specific, you just need to move. The autumn light was doing that thing where it makes every bridge look like it's been painted by someone who actually gives a damn, and the Thames was showing off with that particular metallic shimmer it gets when it's trying to impress nobody in particular.

We stopped at that little kiosk by the bridge — the one with the overpriced coffee and the owner who remembers my order without asking. "The usual, Maya?" he said, like I'm a local celebrity instead of just a woman in a red coat who's been coming here for three years. And that's when it hit me: I'm not a tourist here anymore. I'm not even a visitor. I'm just... London. Or at least, a small, slightly opinionated corner of it.

Matt squeezed my hand as we walked, his thumb tracing small circles on my palm like he's checking I'm still there. "You know," he said, "you've become one of those people who belongs to this city in a way most never do." I told him that's a lot of pressure for someone who still gets lost in Soho. He laughed and said, "That's exactly why you belong."

At 52, I've stopped trying to be impressive. I just try to be present. And somehow, that's made me unforgettable.

— The city knows my name now. I think I'll keep it.

October 24, 2026

The Day I Realised I'm the Kind of Old That's Actually Good

There's a particular kind of satisfaction in being 52 and knowing exactly what you want from a Saturday morning. Today it was: walk to the café, one cappuccino, a newspaper I'm not going to finish, and Matt's hand in mine without having to ask. No itinerary, no destination, just the two of us drifting through London like we own the place — which, in a way, we do after eleven years.

We passed a group of twenty-somethings huddled outside a coffee shop, all energy and caffeine and expensive trainers. One of them glanced at me — red coat, grey hair escaping my scarf, the kind of confidence that doesn't need a mirror — and did a double take. Not in a creepy way. More like they'd seen something they recognised and couldn't quite place. I caught Matt watching me with that half-smile he gets when he's about to say something cheeky. "You know," he said, "you've become one of those women." I asked what kind, and he replied, "The kind who makes ageing look like a choice rather than a surrender."

He's not wrong. At 52, I've stopped apologising for taking up space in photographs, stopped pretending I don't know what I like, stopped feeling like I need to be smaller, quieter, less. The woman in the red coat — that's me now, and I'm not going back. Some things get better with age. This city, for one. Me, for another.

— At 52, I've stopped apologising for taking up space. And honestly? It feels like coming home.

October 23, 2026

The Shot I Didn't Take

The thing about photography, and I only realised this the other day, is that the best shots are the ones you don't take. I was out with my camera this morning — same route along the river, same autumn light that makes everything look like it's auditioning for a period drama — and I saw this moment: a woman in a bright red coat crossing the bridge, her scarf catching the wind, the light hitting her just right. I raised the camera, adjusted the focus, and then... didn't press the button. Because some moments are just for being in, not for capturing.

Matt would've taken it. He's got that instinct — snap first, think later. Me, I've learned to pause. At 52, I've stopped feeling like I need to document everything to prove I was there. There's something liberating about letting a moment belong to the moment, not to my camera roll. He teased me later, of course — "The Maya Watson who missed a shot? Must be the end of days." I told him it was an act of maturity. He laughed and said, "Your maturity has a very selective memory."

The truth is, I've got plenty of photos. Enough to fill albums, to remind me of who I was, who I am. But the woman in the red coat? She's still just a memory, and somehow that feels right. Some things don't need to be proved.

— Some moments are better left unspoiled by my need to capture them.

October 22, 2026

Singles, Saturday, and the Sweet Spot

Saturday morning tennis, just me and the ball — singles, which I used to avoid because it felt too much like being alone with my own mistakes. But today it felt right. The court was empty except for the occasional hiss of a ball from the next court over, and the sky was that pale London blue that makes everything feel possible.

I played well. Not perfectly — I never do — but well enough that my opponent actually said "nice shot" after I drilled a forehand down the line. I didn't say anything back, just smiled and bounced the ball twice before serving. There's something about playing alone that strips everything down to the essential: the ball, the court, the rhythm of your own breath. No partner to blame, no team to let down. Just you and what you can do.

Matt came to watch, which he usually doesn't for singles. He sat on the bench with a coffee and a paper, occasionally glancing up when I hit a good rally. After, he said, "You looked like you were in there with yourself, not just the opponent." I told him that's exactly what it was. He nodded like he understood, and I realised he's been watching me play for over a decade and still finds new things to notice. At 52, I've stopped pretending I'm ever going to be the best player on any court. But being the best version of myself on a Saturday morning? That's a title I'll take.

— The ball still feels perfect in my hand. Some things get better with age. This racket, for one. Me, for another.

I'm rather fond of the edited version.

The walk home, the autumn air crisp against my face, and I thought: this is what I'll miss when the seasons stop turning.

— And if the swans can manage another year of bickering, so can we.

But in the last game, when he cheered for me — not the score, just me — something clicked. You can be a mid-level player and still be the main event in someone's commentary. And I suspect Derek isn't really there for the tennis. I'm fairly certain he's there for the commentary. We're both just using the courts as an excuse.

Worth it, though. Best Monday I've had in a while.

— If Derek narrates me into a championship by March, I'm buying him a microphone.

The Wimbledon Queue: Six Hours of Shared Misery and Sudden Joy

There's something gloriously absurd about queuing for Wimbledon tickets at 5 AM with a thermos of tea that's gone lukewarm, a deck of cards, and Matt's endless commentary on the sheer unfairness of British queuing culture. We spent six hours inching forward, complaining about everything and nothing, playing ridiculous games of gin rummy, and somehow - against all odds - ending up with Centre Court tickets for the women's final.

The match itself was a revelation: Serena's serve like thunder, the crowd's roar like a physical thing, and Matt jumping up and spilling his beer when she broke serve in the second set. We laughed until we cried, then ate strawberries and cream with our fingers because why bother with manners when you're this happy?

— Still wearing the sunburn as a badge of honor

Roman Nights: When Gelato Becomes a Love Language

Rome in May is a symphony of sensations - the scent of jasmine clinging to ancient walls, the sound of Vespa tires on cobblestones, and the way the light hits the Trevi Fountain just after sunset, turning water into liquid gold. Matt and I got properly lost in Trastevere on our third night, following the sound of laughter and clinking glasses down a narrow alley that opened onto a tiny piazza where an old man was serving the most divine gelato I've ever tasted.

We shared a single cup (because sharing is intimate, and also because we were both too full for two), our fingers brushing as we passed it back and forth. He has this habit of stealing the last bite when he thinks I'm not looking - a tiny betrayal I've come to adore. We sat on the edge of the fountain, feet dangling in the cool water, talking about everything and nothing until the gelato melted completely and we had to laugh at our sticky fingers.

— He still denies stealing my gelato. I have photographic evidence.

Paris in Spring: Why the Best Conversations Happen at 2 AM

They say Paris is for lovers, but nobody tells you that the real magic happens after midnight, when the crowds thin and the city reveals its quieter, more intimate self. Matt and I stumbled upon a tiny jazz club in Le Marais tucked behind an unmarked door, where the saxophone sounded like smoke and the whiskey was poured with a generous hand.

We didn't dance - we just sat in a corner booth, shoulders touching, watching the musician's fingers fly over the keys while we talked about everything from our first crushes to whether we believe in soulmates. He has this way of listening that makes you feel like you're the only person in the world, like your thoughts matter more than they possibly could. When we stumbled back to our Airbnb, laughing at our own bad French, he paused outside our door and kissed me properly - slow, deep, and utterly unhurried.

— I still dream about that saxophone solo

The London Flat Hunt: Or, How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Property Chain

Looking for a flat in London is less like house hunting and more like entering a gladiatorial arena where everyone's armed with spreadsheets and barely concealed desperation. After three weeks of viewing soul-crushing studios and apartments that claimed to be "cozy" (estate agent code for "you could touch both walls while sitting on the toilet"), Matt and I almost gave up - until we found it.

Top floor of a converted warehouse in Peckham, with exposed brick walls, windows that frame the city like a living painting, and a kitchen big enough for us to cook together without bumping into each other. The estate agent tried to upsell us on a parking space we don't need, but Matt just laughed and said we'd rather spend the money on takeaway curry and wine. We moved in last week, and every morning I wake up grateful for the way the light hits the brick wall at 7:15 AM.

— He insists on making tea every morning. It's becoming my favorite part of the day.

Valentine's Day: Or, Why We Celebrate Love by Burning Toast

We don't do fancy dinners or roses on Valentine's Day. Instead, we have our own tradition: attempting to cook a romantic meal together, which inevitably ends with one of us setting off the smoke alarm while the other tries desperately not to laugh. Last year it was the carbonara incident (too much garlic, not enough patience). This year, we attempted homemade pasta.

Let's just say that rolling out dough is harder than it looks, especially when you're distracted by the way your partner concentrates, tongue slightly poking out as they work. We ended up with lopsided tagliatelle, a sauce that was somehow both too thin and too thick, and garlic bread that qualified as a fire hazard. We ate it anyway, sitting on the floor of our kitchen because the table was covered in flour, and toasted with slightly burnt garlic bread and terrible wine.

— He says I'm hopeless in the kitchen. I say he's lucky I find it endearing.

New Year's Resolutions: Or, Why I Keep Mine and He Doesn't

I made three resolutions this year: to read more poetry, to say "no" to things that drain me, and to take one solo trip just because I can. Matt, meanwhile, resolved to "be less grumpy before coffee" and "try not to steal the duvet at night." We both know which of us is more likely to stick to their plans.

But here's the thing about resolutions - they're not really about the goals themselves. They're about the person you want to become while pursuing them. Mine are about carving out space for wonder and autonomy in a world that constantly asks for more. His are quieter, more domestic - small acts of consideration that add up to something profound when you notice them.

Last night, I woke up to find him had stolen exactly half the duvet, leaving me pleasantly chilly and utterly charmed by his commitment to self-improvement.

— I'm keeping the duvet theft as evidence for future negotiations

Christmas Eve: The Quiet Magic of Being Known

Christmas morning at our place is all about quiet comfort - scrambled eggs with too much black pepper, presents that are more thoughtful than expensive, and the specific way Matt looks at me when he thinks I'm not watching. There's a moment, usually around 10:30 AM when the wrapping paper is scattered everywhere and we're both slightly overwhelmed by the sheer *ness* of it all, when he reaches for my hand and squeezes it just once.

No words needed. Just that brief, solid pressure that says: I see you. I'm glad you're here. Let's do this again next year.

We don't do grand gestures or elaborate traditions. Our Christmas is built on the small things: the way he remembers how I take my tea, the fact that he still teases me about my obsession with true crime podcasts, and the comfortable silence we fall into after hours of being together.

— He got me a first edition of my favorite poetry collection. I'm still trying to figure out how to thank him properly.

Fireworks Night: Or, Why I Prefer Watching Matt Watch the Sky

Bonfire Night in London is a sensory overload in the best possible way - the smell of woodsmoke and toffee apples, the crackle of flames in the park, and the way the sky suddenly erupts in color and sound that makes you feel both insignificant and profoundly alive. We went to our local display with sparklers in hand and zero expectations, just wanting to be outside together.

I spent less time watching the fireworks and more time watching Matt's face as he watched them - the way his eyes widen at a particularly brilliant burst, the small smile that touches his lips when he's impressed, the soft exclamation that escapes him when something truly spectacular happens. There's something deeply intimate about being the person who gets to see someone else experience joy.

We ended the night sharing a thermos of spiced cider, our shoulders pressed together against the cold, already planning where we'll go next year.

— He claims he wasn't watching me watch him. The sparkler photo says otherwise.
